Letters, vowels, tones and how to type them

Consonants

Every Thai consonant has a name: its sound plus a word that starts with it, the way English children learn “A is for apple.” ก is gɔɔ gài, “g as in chicken.” Since several letters share a sound, the word is what tells them apart. Type the whole name (ko kai, gor gai) or just the word (kai); most common romanizations are accepted. In Letters mode you may instead type the letter’s sounds: the sound at the start of a syllable, then the one at the end (ด is d t). When the two are the same, or the letter isn’t used at the end, type the sound once (ม is m, ฉ is ch). Since many letters share sounds, this is a looser test than the name; “Full letter names only” turns it off. You can add your own shortcuts in the boxes (separate several with commas). Each letter also belongs to a class, mid high or low, which decides the tone of a syllable. In Spell mode, type each consonant of the word, left to right, separated by spaces; vowel symbols and tone marks are skipped.

Vowel symbols

Vowels are written before, after, above or below the consonant (◌ marks where the consonant goes), but are always said after it: เก is gee. Most come in short and long pairs. A syllable with no written vowel says a short o if it ends in a consonant (คน khon), or a short a if it doesn’t (the ส in สบาย sà-baai).

Reading aloud (mode 3)

Type how each syllable sounds, in Latin letters, with spaces or hyphens between syllables: สวัสดี is sa wat dii. Aspirated sounds, with a puff of air, take an h: kh, ph, th, ch. The unaspirated ones are typed k or g (ก), p or bp (ป), t or dt (ต), and j (จ). Double a vowel to make it long (aa, ii, uu, oo); ee works for ii as in English “see”. At the end of a syllable Thai only has the sounds k, t, p, m, n, ng (plus the glides i and o), so ส, ด, จ, ช and friends all end as t, and ร and ล end as n. The answer is shown in Paiboon-style spelling, with ʉ (like “u” said with unrounded lips), ɛ (as in “bed”), ɔ (as in “law”) and ə (as in “the”). You may type those letters too, or ue, ae, or/aw and oe/er.

With Grade tones on, mark each syllable’s tone with a number after it, 1 mid · 2 low · 3 falling · 4 high · 5 rising (sa2 wat2 dii1), or with Paiboon accents (sà wàt dii). A syllable with no mark counts as mid.

Tones

A syllable is live if it has a long vowel or ends in m, n, ng, i or o; it is dead if it ends in k, t or p, or ends in a short vowel. The tone follows from the first consonant’s class, live or dead, and any tone mark:

ห written silently before a low-class ง ญ น ม ย ร ล ว makes it high class (หมู mǔu, ไหน nǎi); อ does the same for ย in four words (อยู่ yùu). A few everyday words break the rules, and so do many words borrowed from English.
